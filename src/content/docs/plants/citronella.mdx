## Origin

The potted plant sold as "Citronella" is not citronella grass (*Cymbopogon*,
the source of commercial citronella oil) but a scented-leaf pelargonium
whose foliage happens to smell like it. 'Citrosum' is a garden hybrid of
South African pelargoniums: sun-hungry, drought-tolerant once established,
and with no idea what to do with frost.

## Care

- **Light** — full sun to bright indirect.
- **Temperature** — 50–86 °F; no frost — overwinter indoors.
- **Watering** — every 5 days; let the top few centimeters dry out first.
- **Fertilizing** — every 4 weeks, April–September; balanced liquid feed
  at half strength.
- **Trimming** — yearly, March–April; prune back hard to keep it bushy.
- **Repotting** — yearly, March–April; fresh mix at the same time as the
  spring prune.

Bring it inside before the first cold night and give it a bright, cool
window for the winter. Cuttings root readily in early autumn, which is
cheaper insurance than hauling a large pot through the winter.

## Pruning

The hard spring cutback is the whole game: in March, as the first new
shoots show, prune it back hard — it gets leggy without — and do the root
work in the same session, shaving a few centimeters off the root ball and
returning it to the same pot with fresh mix. Pelargoniums are extremely
forgiving of this double hit, and the smaller top is exactly what makes
the smaller roots safe.

## Home medicine

The marketing claim — that the plant itself repels mosquitoes — has not
held up in trials; sitting next to one does nothing. Crushing a few leaves
and rubbing them on the skin does release citronellal and gives short-lived,
mild repellent effect, more folk remedy than substitute for a real
repellent. The scented leaves also do honest work in potpourri and drawer
sachets.
