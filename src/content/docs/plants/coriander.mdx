## Origin

Coriander comes from the eastern Mediterranean and western Asia, where it
grows as a cool-season annual: up with the rains, seed before the summer
drought. That biome made it an annual on a deadline — warmth, long days,
or a dry spell all flip it from leaf production to a flower stalk, and
once it bolts the leaves turn sparse and soapy-thin. Racing to seed when
it turns hot and dry is not a flaw but the original strategy.

## Care

- **Light** — bright, with morning sun; shade from midday heat.
- **Temperature** — 50–77 °F; bolts in heat.
- **Watering** — every 2 days; evenly moist — drought stress triggers
  bolting.
- **Fertilizing** — every 4 weeks, March–September; half-strength balanced
  liquid feed.
- **Repotting** — none; an annual that hates transplanting — sow directly
  in the final pot and resow every few weeks.

The pot strategy is not to fight the bolting but to plan for it — sow a
pinch of seed directly in the pot (the taproot resents transplanting,
which is why supermarket pots sulk), harvest for a few weeks, and have the
next pot already sown. In summer heat, morning sun and midday shade
stretch each sowing.

## Pruning

Harvesting *is* the pruning: cut outer leaves and let the center keep
producing, or shear the whole pot at its peak and resow. Once it bolts,
put the scissors away and let it finish — cutting the flower stalk only
buys days, and the seeds that follow are the second harvest.

## In the kitchen

One plant, two spices. The fresh leaves (cilantro) are the raw, last-second
herb of salsa, curry, phở, and guacamole — cooking erases them. If a pot
bolts before you get there, let it: the round seeds that follow are
coriander proper, warm and citrusy, for toasting and grinding into curry
bases and spice rubs. Whether the leaves taste bright or like soap is partly
genetic — if you drew the soap card, the seeds are your consolation prize.
