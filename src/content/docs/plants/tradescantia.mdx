*Tradescantia zebrina* wears its name on its leaves: a pair of shimmering
pink-silver racing stripes over deep purple, with an underside of solid
wine-purple — in good light the whole plant reads purple with the stripes
as highlights. It creeps over the forest floors of southern Mexico as a
ground cover, which is exactly how it behaves in a pot: every stem crawls
to the rim and keeps going, rooting at each joint it touches. It is close
to effortless — average room temperatures, no misting, forgiveness for
missed waterings. The one thing it cannot do is stay put; "inch plant" is
a warning as much as a name.

## Origin

*Tradescantia zebrina* comes from southern Mexico and Central America,
where it creeps over shaded forest floors as a ground cover. That biome
explains the pot: bright but not baking light, average room warmth, and a
plant that never stops crawling toward the rim.

## Care

- **Light** — bright indirect; some gentle sun deepens the purple.
- **Temperature** — 12–27 °C; keep above 10 °C.
- **Watering** — every 5 days; water when the top few centimeters have
  dried — drier in winter.
- **Fertilizing** — every 4 weeks, April–September; half-strength balanced
  liquid feed — overfeeding fades the stripes.
- **Trimming** — every 4 weeks, March–October; pinch long runners back to
  a joint to keep it dense.
- **Repotting** — yearly, March–April; or skip it — a handful of cuttings
  restarts the pot instead.

The color is a light meter: washed-out, greenish leaves with faint stripes
mean too little light, scorched patches mean too much direct sun. A summer
outside in bright shade suits it, but bring it in well before nights turn
cold — and the sap can irritate skin and curious pets, so it hangs best
out of reach.

## Pruning

Pinching is constant, cheerful maintenance: nip long runners back to just
above a joint and the stem branches there, which is the only way to keep
the pot dense instead of bald in the middle with long purple tails.
Nothing is wasted — every pinched tip roots in water or moist mix within
days. There is no root pruning worth doing: when a pot grows tired and
leggy after a year or two, a handful of fresh cuttings planted back into
the same pot replaces it outright. The plant is its own succession plan.
