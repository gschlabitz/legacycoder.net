## Origin

The Tabasco pepper is a cultivar of *Capsicum frutescens*, named for the
Mexican state of Tabasco; the species comes from the tropical Americas.
Unlike most chilis it holds its small pods upright above the foliage,
ripening from pale yellow-green through orange to red — a pot in full
fruit looks decorated. The tropical heritage sets the rules: real warmth,
all-day sun, and instant death at frost.

## Care

- **Light** — full sun, six hours or more.
- **Temperature** — 59–86 °F; dies at frost.
- **Watering** — every 2 days; evenly moist, never waterlogged — daily
  checks in summer heat.
- **Fertilizing** — every 2 weeks, May–September; tomato feed once the
  first flowers set.
- **Trimming** — once, in May; pinch the first tips for a bushier,
  higher-yielding plant.
- **Repotting** — yearly, in May; one plant per 25+ cm pot — or overwinter
  it indoors, since chilis are perennials.

It runs on the tomato schedule: out with them once nights are reliably
warm, fed with them, and finished with them at frost — unless you bring it
in. A chili on a bright windowsill will overwinter, rest, and fruit
earlier the next year.

## Pruning

Pinch the growing tips once while the plant is young — each pinch forks
the stem, and more forks mean more flowers. After that, harvesting is the
pruning: pick pods as they color and the plant keeps producing. If you
overwinter it, cut it back by about a third before it comes inside; it
rests over winter and regrows from the old frame in spring.

## In the kitchen

Fiery, fruity, and prolific — this is the pepper fermented into Tabasco
sauce, but fresh pods do the same work in salsas, marinades, and anything
that wants clean heat. A single plant outpaces most households: string
surplus pods to dry, or pack them in vinegar for a shake-on hot sauce.
